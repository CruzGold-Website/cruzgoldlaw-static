How a U visa lawyer helps

If you were the victim of domestic violence, sexual assault, a serious assault or another listed crime in the United States, and you helped or are willing to help the police or prosecutors, a U visa can give you legal status, a work permit and, after three years, a path to a green card. A U visa lawyer starts with the facts that decide the case: what happened, who investigated it, and whether an official will sign the law enforcement certification the petition needs.

You can apply even if you have no papers today. USCIS can forgive most immigration problems for U applicants on Form I-192, at no fee (USCIS: U visa). The person who committed the crime does not have to be arrested or convicted (U visa law enforcement resource guide).

If you are in danger now, call 911. The National Domestic Violence Hotline answers around the clock at 1-800-799-7233, or you can text START to 88788. In New Jersey, the statewide hotline is 1-800-572-7233.

What a U visa gives you

U status lasts up to four years. The principal gets a work permit automatically on approval, close family members can be included, and after three years in U status you can apply for a green card (USCIS: U visa).

Congress allows only 10,000 U visas a year for principal applicants, and USCIS has reached that cap every year since 2010. The 2026 cap was reached on September 5, 2026, and on October 1, 2026, USCIS began giving final approvals to petitions filed on or before August 31, 2017 (USCIS: Form I-918). Most people therefore wait years for the visa itself. While they wait, USCIS can grant a work permit and deferred action through its bona fide determination.

Who qualifies for a U visa?

You can petition if all four of these are true (INA 101(a)(15)(U))

  • You were the victim of a qualifying crime
  • You suffered substantial physical or mental abuse because of it
  • You have information about the crime
  • You have helped, are helping or are likely to help the police, prosecutors or another authority investigating or prosecuting it

The crime must have happened in the United States, including Indian country, military bases and U.S. territories, or have broken a federal law that can be prosecuted here. You must also be admissible or ask for a waiver (8 CFR 214.14).

Whether the abuse was substantial depends on the injury, how severe the conduct and the harm were, how long it lasted, and whether the harm is permanent or serious. A series of acts can add up even when no single act would. A person who took part in the crime cannot be its victim.

Some family members can petition as indirect victims when the direct victim was killed or is incapacitated: the spouse and children under 21, and also parents and unmarried siblings under 18 if the victim was under 21 when the crime happened. For a victim who was under 16 when the crime first happened, or one who is incapacitated, a parent, guardian or "next friend" can give the information and the help in the victim's place.

What crimes qualify for a U visa?

The law lists these crimes (INA 101(a)(15)(U)(iii)): domestic violence, sexual assault, abusive sexual contact, rape, incest, felonious assault, stalking, kidnapping, abduction, being held hostage, false imprisonment, unlawful criminal restraint, blackmail, extortion, manslaughter, murder, torture, trafficking, involuntary servitude, peonage, slave trade, prostitution, sexual exploitation, female genital mutilation, witness tampering, obstruction of justice, perjury and fraud in foreign labor contracting. An attempt, conspiracy or solicitation to commit any of them also counts.

A crime with a different name in state law still qualifies when its nature and elements are substantially similar to one on the list. The federal guide for police adds that child abuse and elder abuse can count as domestic violence when they meet the state's domestic violence elements (U visa law enforcement resource guide).

How we get the certification signed

Every U petition needs Form I-918 Supplement B, signed by an official who investigated or prosecuted the crime. Without it, USCIS denies the petition (Form I-918 instructions). Police departments, prosecutors and judges can sign, and so can agencies that investigate crimes in their own field, such as child protective services, the EEOC, the Department of Labor and state labor departments. Inside an agency, the head of the agency or a supervisor the head has designated signs.

No arrest, charge or conviction of the offender is needed, the investigation does not have to be open, and an old case can still be certified. Signing is voluntary for the agency, so the request has to make the agency's job easy (U visa law enforcement resource guide).

We start with the police report or case number, find the agency or court that handled the case, and write a request that ties what happened to the crime on the federal list and to the help you gave. Where we send it depends on where the crime happened:

New Jersey

An Attorney General directive requires every state, county and local police agency to have a procedure that processes certification requests within 120 days (New Jersey Attorney General Directive 2018-6). The State Police say they decide within 120 days and have no legal duty to sign.

Philadelphia

The District Attorney's Office, the Police Department and the Department of Human Services each take requests (City of Philadelphia).

Houston

The Harris County District Attorney certifies at its discretion through an online request form (Harris County District Attorney).

The certification must be signed within the six months before the petition is filed, and an older one is rejected, so we have the rest of the petition ready when the signed form comes back. If one office refuses, a prosecutor, a judge who heard the case or an agency such as the Department of Labor may still be able to sign. We also file the personal statement and the work permit application with the petition, because USCIS will not grant the work permit while you wait without them.

The work permit while you wait

Since June 14, 2021, USCIS gives a work permit to petitioners whose case passes its bona fide determination, without waiting for the cap (USCIS: Form I-918). A petition is bona fide when it is complete, with a Supplement B filed within six months of its signature and a personal statement, and USCIS has your background check results (USCIS Policy Manual).

A favorable determination brings a work permit and deferred action together, for four years, and they can be renewed. Only petitioners living in the United States can get one, and a family member's review starts after the principal's. USCIS still uses its discretion: an arrest or conviction for serious crimes, such as murder, rape, trafficking or drug dealing, generally sends the case to a full review instead. Not getting a determination is not a denial. The case moves to review for the waiting list, where an eligible petitioner also receives deferred action and does not build up unlawful presence.

On February 4, 2025, USCIS stopped the faster "streamlined" review it had used since 2023, so every case now gets the standard review. And a DHS proposal of June 5, 2026, would add criminal-conduct factors to discretionary work permits, including these; it is not final (Federal Register).

How much does a U visa cost?

USCIS charges no fee for the U visa petition (Form I-918), the family supplements, the certification form, the first work permit or the later green card application. The Form I-192 waiver, which costs $1,100 for others, is free for U petitioners and their family members. The fees added by H.R. 1 in 2025 do not apply to U filings (fee schedule, Form G-1055).

How long does a U visa take?

USCIS reports that 80% of petitions get their first decision, either a bona fide determination or a waiting list notice, within 23.5 months (USCIS processing times, updated November 20, 2026). The final approval comes when a visa is available under the cap, and in October 2026 that meant petitions filed in 2017.

After three years in U status, the green card application takes about 26.5 months for 80% of cases, and the Form I-929 for a relative who never had U status about 30.5 months. A single case can take longer.

Family members

A principal under 21 can include a spouse, children, parents and unmarried siblings under 18. A principal 21 or older can include a spouse and children (USCIS: U visa). Each family member is added on Supplement A, with the petition or later, and family members do not count against the cap.

  • A child who was under 21 when you filed remains a child after turning 21 while the case is pending.
  • A sibling's age is fixed on the day you filed.
  • The relationship must exist when you file and still exist when USCIS decides and when your relative enters the United States, except for a child born after you file.
  • A relative who committed the crime against you cannot receive status through you.
  • Family members abroad receive the visa at a U.S. consulate and apply for a work permit after they arrive.

For relatives abroad from certain countries, a travel ban stands in the way. Since January 1, 2026, Proclamation 10998 suspends all visas, U visas included, for nationals of 19 countries, among them Haiti, Afghanistan and Iran, and its published exceptions do not mention U visas (State Department).

Does a U visa lead to a green card?

Yes. After three years of continuous physical presence in U status, you can apply for a green card if you have not unreasonably refused to help the authorities and staying is justified for humanitarian, family or public interest reasons (INA 245(m)). One trip abroad of more than 90 days, or trips adding up to more than 180 days, breaks the three years unless the trip helped the investigation or the certifying official says it was justified.

You must still hold U status when you file and stay in the United States until USCIS decides. USCIS weighs your past conduct at this stage, and it generally refuses where there is a serious violent crime, sexual abuse of a child or several drug crimes. You can show your cooperation with a newly signed Supplement B or with your own sworn statement (8 CFR 245.24). Leaving the country while the green card application is pending abandons it unless you first get advance parole.

U visas and immigration court

Only USCIS decides U petitions, so a person in immigration court files with USCIS, and ICE's lawyers may agree to end the court case while USCIS decides. If you already have a final removal order, filing does not stop ICE from carrying it out, and you must ask for a stay of removal. A favorable bona fide determination counts as the prima facie case the law requires for that stay (8 CFR 214.14(c)(1); USCIS Policy Manual). Once you receive U status, the judge generally must end a pending case, unless the charges against you would have applied even if you had held U status from the start (8 CFR 1003.18).

Courts have become stricter about waiting for USCIS. In 2026 the Board of Immigration Appeals held that a pending U petition no longer creates a presumption of a continuance, and that a judge should not set a case aside over DHS's objection when no U visa is available now or soon (Matter of L. Ramirez; Matter of Ibarra-Vega). ICE has replaced its 2021 policy of holding off enforcement against crime victims with interim guidance: officers must now consult ICE's lawyers before acting against known U applicants, but a pending petition is no shield on its own (ICE guidance 11005.4).

Immigration courts take cases by the DHS office that filed them and by detention center, not by county (EOIR). Is a U visa petition confidential?

Federal law bars immigration officials from disclosing information about a U petitioner, with narrow exceptions, and from finding you deportable or inadmissible based only on information from the person who committed the crime (8 U.S.C. 1367). An official who breaks the rule willfully faces discipline and a fine of up to $5,000. Since December 22, 2025, USCIS no longer applies the second protection when the petitioner has a conviction listed among the crimes that make someone deportable (USCIS policy alert).

Why are U visa petitions denied?

No valid certification

It is missing, or it was signed more than six months before filing.

Abuse not shown as substantial

The petition has to describe the harm and back it with credible evidence, which can be your own signed statement as well as any records you have.

A crime that does not match the list

The facts must fit a listed crime or one substantially similar.

Refusing reasonable requests for help

Cooperation continues after the petition is filed and after approval.

A problem USCIS will not waive

For violent or dangerous crimes, USCIS grants the waiver only in extraordinary circumstances.

A withdrawn certification

The agency can withdraw it later, and USCIS can then revoke the approval or the work permit.

A denied petition can be appealed to USCIS's Administrative Appeals Office, at no fee for U petitioners. Once a denial is final, any stay of removal ends and DHS may start a court case.

Questions about U visas

Does a U visa protect you from deportation?

A pending petition does not stop ICE, and with a final order you must ask for a stay of removal. A favorable bona fide determination brings deferred action, an approval cancels a removal order issued by DHS, and an order from an immigration judge can be reopened and ended on a motion (8 CFR 214.14).

Does the offender have to be convicted?

No. The certification can be signed with no arrest, charge or conviction, and even when the investigation is closed. What matters is that you were a victim of a qualifying crime and were, are or are likely to be helpful.

Can I apply for a U visa if I am undocumented?

Yes. Lawful status is not a requirement. Past immigration violations, such as entering without inspection, can be forgiven on Form I-192, which is free for U petitioners, and USCIS can waive almost every ground of inadmissibility in a U case (8 CFR 212.17).

Can I apply for a U visa from outside the United States?

Yes, if the crime happened in the United States, or happened abroad but broke a federal law that U.S. federal courts can prosecute even when the crime took place in another country. Fingerprints are taken at a U.S. embassy or consulate, and an approved petitioner enters with a visa. Petitioners abroad cannot get the work permit while they wait.

Can a witness to a crime get a U visa?

Usually only if the crime harmed you directly. The federal guide for police says a bystander who suffered an unusually direct injury may qualify, and so can a person targeted by witness tampering, obstruction of justice or perjury meant to escape justice or to keep abusing them through the courts.

You hear our fee at the free consultation, before you decide, and most USCIS work on a U visa case is one flat fee. USCIS itself charges nothing for the U visa forms.
