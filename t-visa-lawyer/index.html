How a T visa lawyer helps

Traffickers keep people working through threats, debts and fear of the authorities. The T visa was written for that situation: if someone forced, tricked or pressured you into work or commercial sex, and that is part of why you are in the United States today, it lets you stay, work and later apply for a green card. A T visa lawyer starts with three questions: what was done to you and how you got out, whether you have told the police or another agency, and what you fear if you had to go back.

You do not need a conviction or a signature from the police, and trafficking does not require crossing a border. A person who agreed to be smuggled can still become a trafficking victim when the smuggler later demands work or sex through force, fraud or coercion (USCIS Policy Manual). Being paid does not mean the work was not forced (USCIS Policy Manual).

If you are in danger now, call 911. The National Human Trafficking Hotline answers around the clock in more than 200 languages at 1-888-373-7888 (TTY 711), or you can text 233733.

Status, work and a green card through the T visa

An approved T visa gives you up to four years of legal status, with a work permit that comes with the approval. Your close family can be included, and so can relatives who are in danger because you escaped or cooperated (USCIS: T visa). After three years in T status, or sooner if the Justice Department certifies that the investigation or prosecution is complete, you can apply for a green card (USCIS: green card for a trafficking victim).

Applications have multiplied, from 3,070 in fiscal year 2022 to 37,177 in fiscal year 2025, and 81,695 principal applications were pending at the end of June 2026 (USCIS data). The same data show approvals below the annual limit of 5,000 T visas in every fiscal year since 2008; the most was 3,786, in fiscal year 2024. During the wait, a bona fide determination can bring a work permit and deferred action.

What counts as human trafficking?

Federal law protects victims of two kinds of severe trafficking (22 U.S.C. 7102):

  • Labor trafficking. Someone recruited, moved, housed or obtained you for work through force, fraud or coercion, to hold you in forced service, debt bondage, peonage or slavery.
  • Sex trafficking. Someone recruited, moved, housed, provided or obtained you for a commercial sex act through force, fraud or coercion. Anyone under 18 who was induced into commercial sex is a victim even without force, fraud or coercion.

Coercion includes threats of serious harm, a scheme to make you believe you or someone else would be hurt if you left, and misuse of the legal process, such as threatening to have you deported. Serious harm does not have to be physical: psychological, financial or reputational harm counts when it would push a reasonable person in your place to keep working. Debt bondage is work pledged against a debt when the work is not reasonably credited toward paying it off, or when how long and what kind of work is owed is never limited and defined (8 CFR 214.201).

You can qualify even if you never did the work or the sex act, when you were recruited or moved for that purpose. You can also qualify if you came here legally, because trafficking is a crime against a person whatever their immigration status.

Who qualifies for a T visa?

You can apply if all four of these are true (INA 101(a)(15)(T)):

  • you are or were a victim of a severe form of trafficking
  • you are in the United States because of the trafficking, including if you were allowed in to help an investigation or a court case
  • you have complied with reasonable requests from law enforcement, or an exception applies to you
  • you would suffer extreme hardship involving unusual and severe harm if you were removed

You must also be admissible or ask for a waiver. A person USCIS has substantial reason to believe committed trafficking cannot receive T status.

Being here because of the trafficking. You can still be under the trafficker's control, have been freed by the police, have escaped before police were involved, or be here because of past trafficking that still ties you to this country. There is no filing deadline, however long ago it happened. If you left the United States after the trafficking and came back, you generally qualify only if the return came from continued victimization, new trafficking, an investigation or court case, or trauma treatment you could not get at home (8 CFR 214.207).

Cooperation with law enforcement. At a minimum you report the trafficking to an agency with jurisdiction, and one documented contact can be enough, including a report by phone or online. After that, you only have to comply with reasonable requests, judged by your trauma, your family's safety, your age, whether you had an interpreter or a lawyer, and how much time you were given. If you were under 18 at the time of any act of trafficking, or physical or psychological trauma keeps you from cooperating, you do not have to have any contact with the police at all (8 CFR 214.208).

Extreme hardship. USCIS looks at your age, medical or psychological care you could not get abroad, the effects of the trafficking, the loss of access to U.S. courts, punishment at home for having been trafficked, the risk of being trafficked again, harm from the trafficker abroad, armed conflict and economic harm. Hardship to your relatives counts when it would cause hardship to you (8 CFR 214.209).

These rules come from the 2024 final rule, in effect since August 28, 2024, which also created the work permit while you wait and broadened the definitions of forced service and serious harm (Federal Register).

How we build your statement and evidence

Every application needs a detailed, signed statement in your own words: what was done to you, how you escaped, why you are in the United States, the harm you fear if you return, and your contact with law enforcement or why an exception applies (8 CFR 214.204(c)). USCIS decides whether you are a victim, and it accepts any credible evidence.

We build the statement from a timeline: how you were recruited and by whom, what you were promised, what changed, how you were kept there, and how you got out. The law asks for both a means (force, fraud or coercion) and an end (forced work, debt bondage or commercial sex), so each part of the story has to show one or the other. Around the statement we gather what exists: police and court records, pay stubs and contracts, recruitment ads and messages, photos, news articles, and letters from therapists, doctors, social workers or other victims (8 CFR 214.206).

The law enforcement declaration, Form I-914 Supplement B, is optional and gets no special weight (8 CFR 214.204(e)). An agency can sign it with no charges filed and no open investigation, and unlike the U visa certification it does not expire (T visa law enforcement resource guide). Records of your report or your testimony can prove cooperation instead (USCIS: T visa). In New Jersey, an Attorney General directive requires every police agency to process T requests within 120 days (New Jersey Attorney General Directive 2018-6), and in Philadelphia the District Attorney, the Police Department and the Department of Human Services each sign them (City of Philadelphia). A federal agency such as Homeland Security Investigations or the FBI can also ask for Continued Presence, a two-year permission to stay and work during the investigation that also counts as evidence for the T visa (ICE: Continued Presence).

What we check before filing: a statement that tells the story but never shows the force, fraud or coercion; a report to police with no record of it, or an exception claimed without a letter from a doctor, therapist or advocate; and nothing tying your presence today to the trafficking. We also file the work permit application with the T application, because filing it later slows the work permit down.

Deferred action and a work permit after the bona fide review

Since August 28, 2024, USCIS first checks whether a new T application is bona fide: properly filed and complete, with a signed personal statement and background checks finished. If it is, USCIS may grant deferred action and, if you filed a work permit application with it, a work permit, both for four years. They can be renewed, and USCIS decides each renewal on its own (USCIS Policy Manual).

A bona fide determination also stays any final removal order automatically until a denial becomes final. Family members in the United States get their own review after the principal's case is found bona fide. If the application is not found bona fide, USCIS moves to a full review, and there is no appeal of that step. USCIS does not publish how long the bona fide review takes. A DHS proposal of June 5, 2026, would add criminal-conduct factors to discretionary work permits, including these; it is not final (Federal Register).

How much does a T visa cost?

USCIS charges no fee for the T application (Form I-914), the family and law enforcement supplements, the work permits (first, renewal or replacement), an extension or the later green card application. The Form I-192 waiver, which costs $1,100 for others, is free for T applicants and their family members, and the work permit fees added by H.R. 1 in 2025 do not apply to T categories (fee schedule, Form G-1055).

A trip abroad can cost $1,000: since October 16, 2025, DHS charges that fee each time it grants parole, including on return with advance parole, and its exceptions do not name T holders, although a green card applicant returning from temporary travel is exempt (Federal Register). Talk to us before any trip.

How long does a T visa take?

USCIS reports that 80% of T applications, victims and family members together, are completed within 36 months (USCIS processing times, updated November 20, 2026). The green card application based on T status takes about 43.5 months for 80% of cases. A single case can take longer.

USCIS denied 2,362 principal applications in fiscal year 2025, up from 603 the year before, and from October 2025 through June 2026 it denied more than it approved: 550 denials against 496 approvals (USCIS data).

Bringing your family, including relatives in danger

A principal 21 or older can include a spouse and unmarried children under 21. A principal under 21 can also include parents and unmarried siblings under 18. At any age, you can include parents, unmarried siblings under 18, and the children of a family member with T status, if they face present danger of retaliation because of your escape or your cooperation (USCIS: T visa). That danger can be shown with a statement from law enforcement, your own statement or other credible evidence, even if the police never investigated.

  • Family members are added on Supplement A, with your application, while it is pending, or after you have T status.
  • A child under 21 when you filed stays eligible after turning 21.
  • Relatives abroad receive the visa at a U.S. consulate and apply for a work permit after they arrive.
  • Once you have a green card, a relative who never entered in T status can no longer come as your T family member, so we add relatives early.
  • Since January 1, 2026, Proclamation 10998 suspends all visas for nationals of 19 countries, among them Haiti, Afghanistan and Iran, and its published exceptions do not mention T visas (State Department).

The green card after a T visa

You can apply for a green card after three years of continuous presence in T status, or sooner if the Justice Department certifies that the investigation or prosecution is complete. An application filed before three years without that certification is rejected. One trip of more than 90 days, or trips adding up to more than 180 days, breaks the three years unless the trip was needed for the investigation or an official involved in it certifies that the absence was justified (INA 245(l)).

You also need good moral character since you were admitted in T status, shown with your own statement and police clearances, although DHS can overlook a problem the trafficking caused. You must also show continued cooperation with reasonable requests, or extreme hardship, or that you were under 18 at the time, or that the trauma exception was established when T status was granted. Unlike the U visa route, the T green card requires admissibility or a waiver, but the three- and ten-year bars do not apply when the trafficking was a central reason for your unlawful presence (8 CFR 245.23).

Filing for the green card during T status extends the status automatically; not filing before the four years end lets it expire. Since the 2024 rule, a T-1 holder needs advance parole before any trip abroad, and leaving while the green card application is pending abandons it unless advance parole was granted first.

If you are in immigration court

Only USCIS decides T applications, so a person in immigration court files with USCIS, and DHS may agree to a joint motion to close or end the court case while USCIS decides (USCIS Policy Manual). With a final removal order, filing does not stop removal by itself; you can ask ICE for a stay, and a bona fide determination stays the order automatically. USCIS generally does not refer a T applicant to immigration court while the case is pending or after a denial, unless there are serious aggravating circumstances such as an egregious criminal record or a role in trafficking. Once you receive T status, the judge generally must end a pending case, unless the charges against you would still apply even if you had held T status from the start, as with some criminal charges (8 CFR 1003.18).

ICE has replaced its 2021 policy of holding off enforcement against crime victims with interim guidance. Officers should now consult ICE's lawyers before acting against known T applicants, and information from the trafficker must be independently corroborated before it is used against you (ICE guidance 11005.4).

Immigration courts take cases by the DHS office that filed them and by detention center, not by county (EOIR). Will the trafficker find out?

Federal law bars immigration officials from disclosing information about a T applicant outside the agencies that need it, with narrow exceptions, and from finding you inadmissible or deportable based only on information from the trafficker, unless you have a conviction listed among the crimes that make someone deportable (8 U.S.C. 1367). An official who breaks the rule willfully faces discipline and a fine of up to $5,000. Since December 22, 2025, USCIS checks first for such a conviction, and it does not apply the limit on the trafficker's information where it finds one or where it is not deciding whether you are inadmissible or deportable (USCIS policy alert).

Help under state law in New Jersey, Pennsylvania and Texas

The three states where we have offices give trafficking victims their own remedies in state court:

  • New Jersey. A victim can ask the Superior Court to vacate and expunge convictions for offenses committed because of the trafficking, except the most serious crimes (N.J.S.A. 2C:44-1.1). Being a trafficking victim is a defense to a prostitution charge (N.J.S.A. 2C:34-1), and victims can sue the traffickers (N.J.S.A. 2C:13-8.1) and apply for victim compensation (N.J.S.A. 52:4B-11).
  • Pennsylvania. A victim can move to vacate convictions for trespass, disorderly conduct, loitering, prostitution or simple drug possession committed as a direct result of being trafficked, with the prosecutor's consent, and the records are expunged (18 Pa.C.S. 3019). Courts may not disclose a victim's identity, and victims can sue anyone who took part in the trafficking.
  • Texas. A victim convicted of certain misdemeanors or prostitution committed solely as a victim, who helped law enforcement when asked or could not because of age or a disability caused by the trafficking, can ask for an order of nondisclosure once a year has passed since the sentence ended. The order keeps criminal justice agencies from releasing the record to the public (Tex. Gov't Code 411.0728).

We look at how any record affects your immigration case before you use one of these remedies.

Why are T visa applications denied?

A denial can come from the gaps we check for before filing: force, fraud or coercion never shown, no proof of a report or of an exception, and nothing tying your presence today to the trafficking. Two more causes are hardship on removal described without proof, and a ground of inadmissibility USCIS will not waive. Grounds other than health must be connected to the trafficking, and for violent or dangerous crimes USCIS grants the waiver only in extraordinary circumstances unless the trafficking caused the crime (8 CFR 212.16).

A denial can be appealed or reopened on Form I-290B at no fee for T applicants. Once a denial is final, the work permit and deferred action end, a stay is lifted, and DHS may start a court case.

  1. The free consultation. Zachary hears what happened, at your pace, and tells you whether the T visa or another route fits.
  2. The report and the evidence. Your report to law enforcement is documented, or the exception is, and the records are gathered.

Questions about T visas

What is the difference between a T visa and a U visa?

The T visa is for trafficking victims; the U visa covers a longer list of crimes, from domestic violence to extortion. A U visa needs a signed certification from law enforcement, while for a T visa that declaration is optional and one documented report can be enough. The U visa cap of 10,000 a year is reached every year, so U petitioners wait years for the visa itself, while T approvals have stayed below the limit of 5,000 in every year of USCIS data since 2008. A T holder can also apply for a green card before three years once the Justice Department certifies that the investigation or prosecution is complete.

Do I have to testify against the trafficker?

Not necessarily. You must comply only with reasonable requests from law enforcement, judged by your trauma, your safety and your family's, and your age. Victims who were under 18 at the time, and those whose trauma keeps them from cooperating, are excused.

Can I apply if the trafficking happened years ago?

Yes. There is no filing deadline. You still have to show that you are in the United States today because of the trafficking, for example because of its continuing effects on you.

Can I get a T visa if I came to the United States legally?

Yes. Trafficking does not depend on how you entered: someone can arrive on a valid visa and be trafficked after arriving.

Can my family come to the United States?

Yes, unless a travel ban applies. Your spouse and children, and in some cases your parents and siblings, can be included and receive their visas at a U.S. consulate. Visas are suspended for nationals of 19 countries, including Haiti, Afghanistan and Iran, with no published exception for T visas. At any age, you can include parents, young siblings and the children of family members if they are in danger of retaliation.

How much does a T visa lawyer cost?

You hear our fee at the free consultation, before you decide, and most USCIS work on a T visa case is one flat fee. USCIS itself charges nothing for the T visa forms.
